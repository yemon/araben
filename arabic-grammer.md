# Arabic Sentence Formation and Grammar: A Beginner's Map

This is a working reference, not a textbook. Read it once end to end to get the shape of the language. Then come back to individual sections as you need them.

Everything here is **Modern Standard Arabic (MSA / الفصحى al-fuṣḥā)**, the written and formal register used in books, news, and official documents across the Arab world.

---

## Part 0: Before Grammar, Three Orientation Facts

### 0.1 Which Arabic?

| Variety | Where it lives | Should a beginner start here? |
|---|---|---|
| Classical Arabic | Quran, classical literature, heritage texts | Same grammar as MSA, older vocabulary |
| Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) | News, books, formal speech, all writing | **Yes. Start here.** |
| Dialects (Egyptian, Levantine, Gulf, etc.) | Daily conversation | Later, and pick one based on need |

MSA and Classical Arabic share essentially the same grammar. Learn MSA and classical texts open up to you. Dialects drop most case endings and change some vocabulary, so they get easier once the standard grammar is in place.

### 0.2 The Script in One Paragraph

Arabic runs right to left. There are 28 letters, all consonants, and most connect to the letters beside them, so a single letter can have up to four shapes depending on its position. Six letters (ا د ذ ر ز و) never connect to the letter after them, which is why words seem to break in the middle. Short vowels are not letters. They are optional marks written above and below the consonants, and they are usually left out in normal text. Adults read unvowelled text by recognising word patterns, which is exactly what grammar teaches you to do.

### 0.3 The Vowel Marks (Ḥarakāt)

| Mark | Name | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| َ | fatḥa | short a | بَ = ba |
| ِ | kasra | short i | بِ = bi |
| ُ | ḍamma | short u | بُ = bu |
| ْ | sukūn | no vowel | بْ = b |
| ّ | shadda | double the consonant | بّ = bb |
| ً ٍ ٌ | tanwīn | -an, -in, -un (indefinite ending) | بٌ = bun |

Long vowels use actual letters: ا (ā), و (ū), ي (ī).

## Part 1: The Foundation

### 1.1 Every Arabic Word Is One of Three Things

Classical Arabic grammar divides the entire vocabulary into three buckets. This is not trivia. It determines which rules apply.

| Type | Arabic | What it covers | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Noun | اِسْم (ism) | nouns, adjectives, pronouns, names, participles | بَيْت (bayt) house |
| Verb | فِعْل (fiʿl) | actions, carries tense | كَتَبَ (kataba) he wrote |
| Particle | حَرْف (ḥarf) | prepositions, conjunctions, question words, negators | فِي (fī) in |

Notice that adjectives are nouns in Arabic thinking. That is why an adjective behaves so much like the noun it describes.

### 1.2 The Root System

This is the single most useful thing to understand early. Most Arabic words are built from a three-consonant root that carries a core meaning. Vowels and extra letters are then poured into that root using fixed patterns.

Take the root **ك ت ب (k-t-b)**, which means *writing*:

| Word | Transliteration | Meaning | Pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| كَتَبَ | kataba | he wrote | faʿala |
| كِتَاب | kitāb | book | fiʿāl |
| كَاتِب | kātib | writer | fāʿil |
| مَكْتَب | maktab | desk, office | mafʿal (place) |
| مَكْتَبَة | maktaba | library, bookshop | mafʿala (place) |
| مَكْتُوب | maktūb | written | mafʿūl (passive participle) |
| كِتَابَة | kitāba | the act of writing | fiʿāla |

Once you know 40 or 50 patterns, you can meet a completely new word and guess both its meaning and its grammatical role. Dictionaries are also organised by root, not alphabetically by the word as written, so this is a practical necessity.

Grammarians use ف ع ل (f-ʿ-l) as the dummy root for describing patterns. That is why you see faʿala, fāʿil, mafʿūl everywhere.

### 1.3 Definiteness

Arabic has no word for "a" or "an". A bare noun is indefinite by default.

- بَيْتٌ (baytun) = a house
- الْبَيْتُ (al-baytu) = the house

The definite article is **الـ (al-)**, prefixed directly onto the word. Two things happen when you add it:

1. The tanwīn ending disappears. `baytun` becomes `al-baytu`.
2. With 14 "sun letters" the l is not pronounced and the next consonant doubles instead. الشَّمْس is written *al-shams* but pronounced *ash-shams*.

**Sun letters:** ت ث د ذ ر ز س ش ص ض ط ظ ل ن
**Moon letters:** everything else, where the l is pronounced normally (الْقَمَر = al-qamar)

### 1.4 Gender

Every noun is either masculine or feminine. There is no neuter.

- Default is masculine.
- The main feminine marker is **ة (tāʾ marbūṭa)** at the end: مُعَلِّم (muʿallim, male teacher) → مُعَلِّمَة (muʿallima, female teacher).
- Some words are feminine without the marker, usually by convention or logic: أُمّ (umm, mother), شَمْس (shams, sun), أَرْض (arḍ, earth), نَار (nār, fire), and most country and city names.
- Paired body parts are feminine: يَد (yad, hand), عَيْن (ʿayn, eye).

### 1.5 Number: Singular, Dual, Plural

Arabic has a separate dual form. This is unusual and worth internalising early.

| Number | Ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Singular | base form | كِتَابٌ kitābun (a book) |
| Dual | ـَانِ / ـَيْنِ | كِتَابَانِ kitābāni (two books) |
| Plural | varies | كُتُبٌ kutubun (books) |

Plurals come in two kinds:

**Sound plurals** keep the word intact and add an ending.

| Type | Ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sound masculine | ـُونَ / ـِينَ | مُعَلِّمُونَ muʿallimūna (teachers, m) |
| Sound feminine | ـَات | مُعَلِّمَات muʿallimāt (teachers, f) |

**Broken plurals** rearrange the word internally. These are the majority and must be memorised with each noun, the way German learners memorise the article.

| Singular | Plural | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| كِتَاب kitāb | كُتُب kutub | book / books |
| رَجُل rajul | رِجَال rijāl | man / men |
| بَيْت bayt | بُيُوت buyūt | house / houses |
| وَلَد walad | أَوْلَاد awlād | boy / boys |
| قَلَم qalam | أَقْلَام aqlām | pen / pens |

### 1.6 Case Endings (الإعراب al-iʿrāb)

Arabic marks a noun's grammatical job with a vowel on its final letter. English uses word order for this; Arabic uses endings, which is why word order can be flexible.

| Case | Arabic | Definite ending | Indefinite ending | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | رَفْع rafʿ | -u ( ُ ) | -un ( ٌ ) | subject, predicate |
| Accusative | نَصْب naṣb | -a ( َ ) | -an ( ً ) | direct object, adverbs |
| Genitive | جَرّ jarr | -i ( ِ ) | -in ( ٍ ) | after any preposition, second part of a possessive |

Compare:

- الْوَلَدُ (al-waladu) the boy, as subject
- الْوَلَدَ (al-walada) the boy, as object
- الْوَلَدِ (al-waladi) the boy, after a preposition

In everyday speech these endings are dropped, and modern texts rarely write them. You still need to know them because they are how you parse a sentence correctly, and they are always pronounced in formal recitation, news broadcasts, and poetry.

## Part 2: The Two Sentence Types

This is the heart of Arabic sentence formation. Every Arabic sentence is one of two kinds, and the label depends on the **first word**.

---

### 2.1 The Nominal Sentence (الجُمْلَة الاِسْمِيَّة)

A sentence that begins with a noun. It has two parts:

| Part | Arabic | Role | Case |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subject | مُبْتَدَأ mubtadaʾ | the thing being talked about | nominative |
| Predicate | خَبَر khabar | the information given about it | nominative |

**Critical rule: there is no verb "to be" in the present tense.** You simply place the two nouns side by side and the "is" is understood.

| Arabic | Transliteration | English |
|---|---|---|
| الْبَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ | al-baytu kabīrun | The house is big. |
| الطَّالِبُ مُجْتَهِدٌ | aṭ-ṭālibu mujtahidun | The student is hardworking. |
| مُحَمَّدٌ مُهَنْدِسٌ | Muḥammadun muhandisun | Muhammad is an engineer. |
| أَنَا مِنْ بَنْغْلَادِيش | anā min Banglādīsh | I am from Bangladesh. |
| هُوَ فِي الْمَكْتَبِ | huwa fī l-maktabi | He is in the office. |

The pattern is normally **definite subject + indefinite predicate**. That contrast is what signals "X is Y" rather than "the Y X".

Watch this closely, because it trips up almost every beginner:

| Arabic | Meaning | Why |
|---|---|---|
| الْبَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ | The house is big. | definite + indefinite = a sentence |
| الْبَيْتُ الْكَبِيرُ | the big house | definite + definite = a phrase, not a sentence |
| بَيْتٌ كَبِيرٌ | a big house | indefinite + indefinite = a phrase |

The khabar does not have to be a single word. It can be a prepositional phrase (فِي الْبَيْتِ, in the house), a whole verbal sentence, or another nominal sentence.

---

### 2.2 The Verbal Sentence (الجُمْلَة الفِعْلِيَّة)

A sentence that begins with a verb. The default order is **Verb – Subject – Object**.

| Part | Arabic | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | فِعْل fiʿl | n/a |
| Doer | فَاعِل fāʿil | nominative |
| Object | مَفْعُول بِهِ mafʿūl bihi | accusative |

| Arabic | Transliteration | English |
|---|---|---|
| كَتَبَ الْوَلَدُ الدَّرْسَ | kataba l-waladu d-darsa | The boy wrote the lesson. |
| قَرَأَ الطَّالِبُ الْكِتَابَ | qaraʾa ṭ-ṭālibu l-kitāba | The student read the book. |
| ذَهَبَ مُحَمَّدٌ إِلَى الْمَدْرَسَةِ | dhahaba Muḥammadun ilā l-madrasati | Muhammad went to the school. |
| فَتَحَ الرَّجُلُ الْبَابَ | fataḥa r-rajulu l-bāba | The man opened the door. |

Note that the case endings do the work English does with position. Because الْوَلَدُ carries -u it must be the doer, and الدَّرْسَ carries -a so it must be the object. Swap the words and the meaning stays the same as long as the endings stay.

#### The agreement rule that surprises everyone

When the verb comes **before** the subject, the verb stays **singular**. It only agrees in gender.

| Arabic | English | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ذَهَبَ الْأَوْلَادُ | The boys went. | verb singular, subject plural |
| ذَهَبَتِ الْبَنَاتُ | The girls went. | verb singular feminine |
| الْأَوْلَادُ ذَهَبُوا | The boys went. | subject first, so now the verb is plural |

Full agreement happens only when the subject comes first.

---

### 2.3 Choosing Between the Two

Both are correct. The difference is emphasis.

- Verbal sentence: neutral, action-focused, the default in narrative and news.
- Nominal sentence: topic-focused, used to describe states, define things, or foreground the subject.

## Part 3: Building Phrases

### 3.1 Adjectives (النَّعْت)

Arabic adjectives come **after** the noun and must match it in four ways at once.

**Gender. Number. Case. Definiteness.**

| Arabic | English |
|---|---|
| بَيْتٌ كَبِيرٌ | a big house |
| الْبَيْتُ الْكَبِيرُ | the big house |
| مَدْرَسَةٌ جَدِيدَةٌ | a new school |
| الْمَدْرَسَةُ الْجَدِيدَةُ | the new school |
| فِي الْبَيْتِ الْكَبِيرِ | in the big house |

Notice the last one. The preposition puts the noun in the genitive, and the adjective follows it into the genitive too.

#### The non-human plural rule

Plural nouns that refer to **things or animals** take a **feminine singular** adjective. This is not optional and it is everywhere.

| Arabic | English | Literally |
|---|---|---|
| الْكُتُبُ الْجَدِيدَةُ | the new books | the books, the new-one (f.sg) |
| السَّيَّارَاتُ الْكَبِيرَةُ | the big cars | the cars, the big-one (f.sg) |
| الْأَوْلَادُ الْجُدُدُ | the new boys | human plural, so a real plural adjective |

The same rule applies to verbs and pronouns referring to non-human plurals.

### 3.2 The Possessive Construction (الإِضَافَة al-iḍāfa)

Arabic has no word for "of" and no apostrophe-s. It joins two nouns directly.

| Arabic | Transliteration | English |
|---|---|---|
| بَابُ الْبَيْتِ | bābu l-bayti | the door of the house |
| كِتَابُ الطَّالِبِ | kitābu ṭ-ṭālibi | the student's book |
| مُدِيرُ الشَّرِكَةِ | mudīru sh-sharikati | the manager of the company |
| غُرْفَةُ الْمُدِيرِ | ghurfatu l-mudīri | the manager's room |

**Four rules, and they are absolute:**

1. The **first noun** (المُضَاف al-muḍāf) never takes الـ and never takes tanwīn. It takes whatever case the sentence requires.
2. The **second noun** (المُضَاف إِلَيْهِ) is **always genitive**.
3. The whole phrase is definite or indefinite based on the **second** noun. بَابُ بَيْتٍ is "a door of a house"; بَابُ الْبَيْتِ is "the door of the house".
4. **Nothing** goes between them. To say "the big door of the house" you must write بَابُ الْبَيْتِ الْكَبِيرُ and let case endings show which noun the adjective belongs to.

You can chain them: كِتَابُ مُدِيرِ الشَّرِكَةِ (the book of the manager of the company).

### 3.3 Prepositions (حُرُوف الجَرّ)

Every preposition forces the following noun into the genitive.

| Arabic | Sound | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| فِي | fī | in |
| مِنْ | min | from |
| إِلَى | ilā | to |
| عَلَى | ʿalā | on |
| بِـ | bi- | with, by (attached) |
| لِـ | li- | for, to (attached) |
| عَنْ | ʿan | about, from |
| مَعَ | maʿa | with (together) |
| عِنْدَ | ʿinda | at, in the possession of |

Examples: فِي الْبَيْتِ (in the house), مِنَ الْمَدْرَسَةِ (from the school), بِالْقَلَمِ (with the pen).

## Part 4: Pronouns

### 4.1 Independent Pronouns

Used mainly as the subject of a nominal sentence, or for emphasis.

| Person | Arabic | Sound | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st sg | أَنَا | anā | I |
| 2nd sg m | أَنْتَ | anta | you (m) |
| 2nd sg f | أَنْتِ | anti | you (f) |
| 3rd sg m | هُوَ | huwa | he, it |
| 3rd sg f | هِيَ | hiya | she, it |
| 1st pl | نَحْنُ | naḥnu | we |
| 2nd dual | أَنْتُمَا | antumā | you two |
| 2nd pl m | أَنْتُمْ | antum | you (m pl) |
| 2nd pl f | أَنْتُنَّ | antunna | you (f pl) |
| 3rd dual | هُمَا | humā | they two |
| 3rd pl m | هُمْ | hum | they (m) |
| 3rd pl f | هُنَّ | hunna | they (f) |

### 4.2 Attached Pronouns

These suffixes do double duty. Attached to a noun they mean "my, your, his". Attached to a verb or preposition they mean "me, you, him".

| Suffix | Sound | On a noun | On a verb |
|---|---|---|---|
| ـِي | -ī | my | me |
| ـكَ | -ka | your (m) | you (m) |
| ـكِ | -ki | your (f) | you (f) |
| ـهُ | -hu | his | him |
| ـهَا | -hā | her | her |
| ـنَا | -nā | our | us |
| ـكُمْ | -kum | your (pl) | you (pl) |
| ـهُمْ | -hum | their | them |

| Arabic | English |
|---|---|
| كِتَابِي | my book |
| كِتَابُكَ | your book |
| بَيْتُهُ | his house |
| مَدْرَسَتُهَا | her school |
| سَأَلَهُ | he asked him |
| عِنْدِي | I have (literally: at me) |
| مِنْهُ | from him |

A noun with an attached pronoun is a form of iḍāfa, so it also drops الـ and tanwīn. You cannot say الْكِتَابِي.

### 4.3 Saying "I Have"

There is no verb "to have". Use a preposition instead.

| Arabic | Transliteration | English |
|---|---|---|
| عِنْدِي كِتَابٌ | ʿindī kitābun | I have a book. |
| عِنْدَهُ سَيَّارَةٌ | ʿindahu sayyāratun | He has a car. |
| لَهَا أُخْتٌ | lahā ukhtun | She has a sister. |
| هُنَاكَ مُشْكِلَةٌ | hunāka mushkilatun | There is a problem. |

## Part 5: The Verb System

### 5.1 Three Basic Forms

Arabic has fewer tenses than English but marks person, gender, and number inside the verb itself.

| Form | Arabic | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Past | الماضي al-māḍī | completed action | كَتَبَ kataba (he wrote) |
| Present | المضارع al-muḍāriʿ | present or ongoing | يَكْتُبُ yaktubu (he writes / is writing) |
| Imperative | الأمر al-amr | command | اُكْتُبْ uktub (write!) |

Future is just the present with a prefix: سَيَكْتُبُ (sa-yaktubu) or سَوْفَ يَكْتُبُ (sawfa yaktubu), meaning "he will write".

### 5.2 Past Tense Conjugation

The past tense works entirely with **suffixes**. Learn this table and you can conjugate almost any sound verb.

| Pronoun | Arabic | Sound | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| أَنَا | كَتَبْتُ | katabtu | I wrote |
| أَنْتَ | كَتَبْتَ | katabta | you wrote (m) |
| أَنْتِ | كَتَبْتِ | katabti | you wrote (f) |
| هُوَ | كَتَبَ | kataba | he wrote |
| هِيَ | كَتَبَتْ | katabat | she wrote |
| نَحْنُ | كَتَبْنَا | katabnā | we wrote |
| أَنْتُمَا | كَتَبْتُمَا | katabtumā | you two wrote |
| هُمَا | كَتَبَا | katabā | they two wrote (m) |
| أَنْتُمْ | كَتَبْتُمْ | katabtum | you wrote (m pl) |
| أَنْتُنَّ | كَتَبْتُنَّ | katabtunna | you wrote (f pl) |
| هُمْ | كَتَبُوا | katabū | they wrote (m) |
| هُنَّ | كَتَبْنَ | katabna | they wrote (f) |

The dictionary form of a verb is the "he" past form, كَتَبَ. That is the entry you look up.

### 5.3 Present Tense Conjugation

The present uses **prefixes**, sometimes with suffixes as well. The prefixes are the key: أ for I, ن for we, ت for you and she, ي for he and they.

| Pronoun | Arabic | Sound | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| أَنَا | أَكْتُبُ | aktubu | I write |
| أَنْتَ | تَكْتُبُ | taktubu | you write (m) |
| أَنْتِ | تَكْتُبِينَ | taktubīna | you write (f) |
| هُوَ | يَكْتُبُ | yaktubu | he writes |
| هِيَ | تَكْتُبُ | taktubu | she writes |
| نَحْنُ | نَكْتُبُ | naktubu | we write |
| أَنْتُمَا | تَكْتُبَانِ | taktubāni | you two write |
| هُمَا | يَكْتُبَانِ | yaktubāni | they two write |
| أَنْتُمْ | تَكْتُبُونَ | taktubūna | you write (m pl) |
| أَنْتُنَّ | تَكْتُبْنَ | taktubna | you write (f pl) |
| هُمْ | يَكْتُبُونَ | yaktubūna | they write (m) |
| هُنَّ | يَكْتُبْنَ | yaktubna | they write (f) |

Because the verb already encodes the person, you do not need a separate pronoun. أَكْتُبُ on its own is a complete sentence meaning "I write". Adding أَنَا is for emphasis.

### 5.4 Present Tense Moods

The present verb changes its final vowel depending on what precedes it. Three moods:

| Mood | Arabic | Ending | Triggered by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indicative | مَرْفُوع marfūʿ | -u | default |
| Subjunctive | مَنْصُوب manṣūb | -a | أَنْ (that), لِـ (in order to), لَنْ (will not), حَتَّى (until) |
| Jussive | مَجْزُوم majzūm | sukūn | لَمْ (did not), لَا الناهية (do not!), conditional particles |

| Arabic | English |
|---|---|
| يَكْتُبُ | he writes |
| لَنْ يَكْتُبَ | he will not write |
| أُرِيدُ أَنْ أَكْتُبَ | I want to write |
| لَمْ يَكْتُبْ | he did not write |

### 5.5 The Verb Forms (الأَوْزَان)

This is where Arabic gets elegant. A single root can be pushed through ten standard patterns, each one shifting the meaning in a predictable direction.

| Form | Pattern | Typical meaning | Example from ع ل م (knowing) |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | فَعَلَ | base meaning | عَلِمَ ʿalima, he knew |
| II | فَعَّلَ | intensive or causative | عَلَّمَ ʿallama, he taught |
| III | فَاعَلَ | doing to someone | قَاتَلَ qātala, he fought (someone) |
| IV | أَفْعَلَ | causative | أَعْلَمَ aʿlama, he informed |
| V | تَفَعَّلَ | reflexive of II | تَعَلَّمَ taʿallama, he learned |
| VI | تَفَاعَلَ | mutual action | تَعَاوَنَ taʿāwana, they cooperated |
| VII | اِنْفَعَلَ | passive, happening to | اِنْكَسَرَ inkasara, it broke |
| VIII | اِفْتَعَلَ | reflexive, varied | اِجْتَمَعَ ijtamaʿa, he gathered |
| IX | اِفْعَلَّ | colours and defects | اِحْمَرَّ iḥmarra, it turned red |
| X | اِسْتَفْعَلَ | seeking or considering | اِسْتَعْلَمَ istaʿlama, he inquired |

You do not need to master these at the start. Just recognise them, because once you do, your vocabulary multiplies. Knowing كَتَبَ gives you access to اِسْتَكْتَبَ, مُكَاتَبَة, and a dozen more.

### 5.6 Weak Verbs

Roots containing و, ي, or ء behave irregularly, because those letters shift or vanish under certain patterns. قَالَ (qāla, he said) comes from ق و ل. وَجَدَ (wajada, he found) loses its و in the present: يَجِدُ (yajidu). This is a real hurdle, but it comes later. Learn sound verbs first.

## Part 6: Negation and Questions

### 6.1 Negation

Arabic uses a different negator depending on tense and sentence type.

| Negator | Used with | Example | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| لَا | present verb | لَا أَكْتُبُ | I do not write |
| مَا | past verb | مَا كَتَبَ | he did not write |
| لَمْ | jussive present, means past | لَمْ يَكْتُبْ | he did not write |
| لَنْ | subjunctive present, means future | لَنْ يَكْتُبَ | he will not write |
| لَيْسَ | nominal sentence | لَيْسَ الْبَيْتُ كَبِيرًا | the house is not big |
| لَا النَّاهِيَة | prohibition | لَا تَكْتُبْ | do not write! |

لَمْ and مَا both negate the past. لَمْ is more common in formal writing.

### 6.2 Questions

**Yes/no questions** need only a particle at the front. Word order does not change.

| Arabic | English |
|---|---|
| هَلْ أَنْتَ طَالِبٌ؟ | Are you a student? |
| هَلْ كَتَبَ الدَّرْسَ؟ | Did he write the lesson? |
| أَأَنْتَ مُحَمَّدٌ؟ | Are you Muhammad? |

**Question words:**

| Arabic | Sound | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| مَنْ | man | who |
| مَا | mā | what (before a noun) |
| مَاذَا | mādhā | what (before a verb) |
| أَيْنَ | ayna | where |
| مَتَى | matā | when |
| كَيْفَ | kayfa | how |
| لِمَاذَا | limādhā | why |
| كَمْ | kam | how many |
| أَيّ | ayy | which |

| Arabic | English |
|---|---|
| مَا اسْمُكَ؟ | What is your name? |
| أَيْنَ الْكِتَابُ؟ | Where is the book? |
| مَاذَا تَفْعَلُ؟ | What are you doing? |
| كَيْفَ حَالُكَ؟ | How are you? |

Note the question mark is mirrored: **؟**

## Part 7: Two Groups That Change the Case Endings

These two sets of words are famous in Arabic grammar because they take a normal nominal sentence and rearrange its case endings. Learn them as a pair, because they do opposite things.

Start with the base sentence: **الْبَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ** (the house is big), both parts nominative.

### 7.1 Kāna and Her Sisters (كَانَ وَأَخَوَاتُهَا)

These are verbs. They keep the subject nominative and push the predicate into the **accusative**.

كَانَ (was), لَيْسَ (is not), صَارَ (became), أَصْبَحَ (became, in the morning), ظَلَّ (remained), مَا زَالَ (still is), لَيْسَ (is not)

| Arabic | English |
|---|---|
| كَانَ الْبَيْتُ كَبِيرًا | The house was big. |
| لَيْسَ الْبَيْتُ كَبِيرًا | The house is not big. |
| أَصْبَحَ الطَّالِبُ مُعَلِّمًا | The student became a teacher. |

Since Arabic has no present-tense "to be", كَانَ is how you say "was". That makes it one of the most useful verbs in the language.

### 7.2 Inna and Her Sisters (إِنَّ وَأَخَوَاتُهَا)

These are particles. They do the reverse: the subject becomes **accusative** and the predicate stays nominative.

إِنَّ (indeed), أَنَّ (that), لَكِنَّ (but), لِأَنَّ (because), كَأَنَّ (as if), لَعَلَّ (perhaps), لَيْتَ (if only)

| Arabic | English |
|---|---|
| إِنَّ الْبَيْتَ كَبِيرٌ | Indeed, the house is big. |
| أَعْلَمُ أَنَّ الْبَيْتَ كَبِيرٌ | I know that the house is big. |
| لِأَنَّ الطَّالِبَ مُجْتَهِدٌ | because the student is hardworking |

Quick memory hook: **kāna flips the second word, inna flips the first.**

## Part 8: Joining Sentences

| Particle | Sound | Meaning | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| وَ | wa | and | attached to the next word |
| فَ | fa | and then, so | implies sequence or result |
| ثُمَّ | thumma | then, afterwards | a longer gap in time |
| أَوْ | aw | or | |
| لَكِنْ | lākin | but | |
| لِأَنَّ | li-anna | because | followed by accusative subject |
| إِذَا | idhā | if (real condition) | |
| عِنْدَمَا | ʿindamā | when | |
| الَّذِي / الَّتِي | alladhī / allatī | who, which | must match gender and number |

Arabic sentences chain with وَ far more readily than English does. A single classical sentence can run for a paragraph. Modern writing is shorter but still comfortable with long coordinated strings.

Relative clauses need care. The relative pronoun appears **only if the noun is definite**:

- الْكِتَابُ الَّذِي قَرَأْتُهُ = the book that I read
- كِتَابٌ قَرَأْتُهُ = a book that I read (no relative pronoun)

## Part 9: Worked Examples

Break these apart word by word and the system clicks.

### Example 1: Nominal

**الطَّالِبُ الْجَدِيدُ فِي الْمَكْتَبَةِ**
*aṭ-ṭālibu l-jadīdu fī l-maktabati*
The new student is in the library.

| Word | Role | Case | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| الطَّالِبُ | mubtadaʾ | nominative -u | subject of nominal sentence |
| الْجَدِيدُ | adjective | nominative -u | matches its noun in all four ways |
| فِي | preposition | n/a | |
| الْمَكْتَبَةِ | object of preposition | genitive -i | forced by فِي |

The predicate here is the whole prepositional phrase.

### Example 2: Verbal with an iḍāfa

**قَرَأَ الطَّالِبُ كِتَابَ الْمُعَلِّمِ**
*qaraʾa ṭ-ṭālibu kitāba l-muʿallimi*
The student read the teacher's book.

| Word | Role | Case | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| قَرَأَ | verb, past | n/a | opens a verbal sentence |
| الطَّالِبُ | fāʿil | nominative -u | the doer |
| كِتَابَ | mafʿūl bihi + muḍāf | accusative -a | object of the verb, and first part of iḍāfa so no الـ |
| الْمُعَلِّمِ | muḍāf ilayhi | genitive -i | second part of iḍāfa, always genitive |

### Example 3: Negated with kāna

**لَمْ يَكُنِ الْبَيْتُ الْقَدِيمُ كَبِيرًا**
*lam yakuni l-baytu l-qadīmu kabīran*
The old house was not big.

| Word | Role | Note |
|---|---|---|
| لَمْ | negator | forces jussive |
| يَكُنْ | kāna in jussive present | means "was" because of لَمْ |
| الْبَيْتُ | ism kāna | stays nominative |
| الْقَدِيمُ | adjective | follows its noun into the nominative |
| كَبِيرًا | khabar kāna | accusative, pushed there by kāna |

## Part 10: Mistakes Beginners Make

| Mistake | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Looking for "is" | English habit | Present tense "to be" simply does not exist |
| Writing "a" or "an" | English habit | Indefiniteness is shown by tanwīn or by nothing at all |
| Putting the adjective first | English word order | Adjective always follows the noun |
| Saying الْكِتَابُ الطَّالِبِ | mixing up iḍāfa | The first term drops الـ: كِتَابُ الطَّالِبِ |
| Making the verb plural before a plural subject | over-applying agreement | Verb before subject stays singular |
| الْكُتُبُ جَدِيدُونَ | treating book-plurals as human | Non-human plurals take feminine singular: الْكُتُبُ الْجَدِيدَةُ |
| Ignoring case endings entirely | they are invisible in most texts | Learn them anyway; they are how you parse ambiguity |
| Memorising words without their plural | broken plurals are unpredictable | Always learn singular + plural as one unit |
| Learning a verb without its present form | the vowel is unpredictable | Learn كَتَبَ / يَكْتُبُ together |

## Part 11: A Practical Learning Sequence

| Stage | Focus | Rough goal |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Script and sounds | Read any vowelled text aloud, even without understanding |
| 2 | 150 to 200 core nouns with their plurals | Enough material to build with |
| 3 | Nominal sentences | Describe things: X is Y, X is in Y |
| 4 | Adjectives and the four agreements | Build accurate noun phrases |
| 5 | Iḍāfa | Express possession and relationships |
| 6 | Pronouns, attached and independent | Say my, your, his, and "I have" |
| 7 | Present tense of sound verbs | Talk about routine and ongoing action |
| 8 | Past tense | Narrate |
| 9 | Negation and questions | Hold a basic exchange |
| 10 | Kāna and inna families | Read real sentences without getting lost |
| 11 | Verb Forms II to X | Vocabulary starts expanding on its own |
| 12 | Weak verbs, passive voice, full iʿrāb | Approach fluent reading |

Two habits that pay off more than anything else:

1. **Learn every noun with its plural, and every verb with its present form.** The extra thirty seconds at input time saves hours later.
2. **Read vowelled text aloud daily, even before you understand it.** Pattern recognition is what eventually lets you read unvowelled text, and it only develops through exposure.

## Quick Reference Card

**Sentence types**
- Nominal: noun + noun, both nominative, no "is"
- Verbal: verb + doer (nominative) + object (accusative)

**Three cases**
- -u nominative: subject, predicate
- -a accusative: object, after inna, khabar kāna
- -i genitive: after prepositions, second term of iḍāfa

**Adjectives** follow the noun and match in gender, number, case, definiteness

**Iḍāfa**: first noun bare, second noun genitive, nothing between them

**Non-human plurals** take feminine singular agreement

**Verb before subject** stays singular

**Negators**: lā (present), mā / lam (past), lan (future), laysa (nominal)

**Kāna** makes the predicate accusative. **Inna** makes the subject accusative.
